Hiring your first YouTube video editor is one of the biggest leaps a creator makes. Done well, it gives you back ten or more hours a week and makes your videos better. Done badly, it costs you money, missed uploads and a lot of frustration.

This guide walks you through the whole process: deciding what you need, choosing between a freelancer and an agency, where to look, how to test candidates, and how to set the relationship up so it lasts.

## Step 1: Decide what you actually need

Before you post a job or contact anyone, get clear on four things.

**Volume.** How many videos a month, and what length? One 10-minute video a week is a very different job from daily Shorts or a two-hour podcast.

**Style.** Collect three to five videos — yours or other creators' — that represent the editing style you want. This is the single most useful thing you can give an editor.

**Scope.** Do you only need cutting, or also thumbnails, captions, Shorts cut-downs, uploading and channel strategy? Bundling tasks can save money, but only if one person or team can genuinely do them all well.

**Budget.** Have a realistic range in mind. If you're unsure, our [YouTube video editing cost guide](/blog/youtube-video-editing-cost) explains typical prices for each level.

Write these down in a one-page brief. You'll reuse it with every candidate.

## Step 2: Freelancer or agency?

Both can be excellent. The right choice depends on your volume, budget and how much management you want to do.

### Hiring a freelancer

**Pros**

- Usually the lowest cost for a given skill level.
- Direct communication with the person editing.
- Flexible — easy to start small.

**Cons**

- Capacity limits: one person can only edit so many hours a week.
- Single point of failure. Illness, holidays or a bigger client can stall your channel.
- You manage quality control, deadlines and briefs yourself.
- Skills are often narrow — a great cutter may not design thumbnails or do motion graphics.

### Hiring an agency

**Pros**

- Reliability. If one editor is unavailable, another steps in with the same style guide.
- Built-in project management and quality control.
- A wider skill set: editors, motion designers, thumbnail designers, colourists and strategists.
- Easier to scale up or down.

**Cons**

- Higher price per video than a solo freelancer at the same level.
- You may not always speak directly to the editor (ask about this upfront).
- Quality varies between agencies just as it does between freelancers.

### A simple rule of thumb

If you publish one video a week or less and enjoy being hands-on, a strong freelancer is often perfect. If you publish multiple times a week, run several formats (long-form, Shorts and podcasts) or can't afford a missed upload, an agency's redundancy is usually worth the premium.

## Freelancer vs team vs agency: side by side

There's a third option people often forget: building a small team yourself, usually one lead editor plus a second editor or a thumbnail designer. Here is how the three compare in practice.

| | Solo freelancer | Your own small team | Agency or studio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Typical cost | Lowest per video | Medium, plus your management time | Highest per video, lowest management time |
| Reliability | Depends on one person | Better — one person can cover another | Built-in backups and deadlines |
| Skills | Usually one specialism | What you hire for | Editing, motion, colour, sound and thumbnails under one roof |
| Management | You brief and review everything | You manage people and workflow | One point of contact manages it |
| Scaling up | Hard — one person's hours | Hire again | Add volume on the same plan |
| Best for | Testing outsourcing, small budgets | Large channels with an operations person | Busy creators and brands who want output, not a hiring project |

### When building your own team makes sense

A small in-house or contract team works when you publish a lot, have someone who can manage people, and want editors who work only on your channel. You'll handle hiring, onboarding, style guides, file storage and cover for holidays yourself. Budget time for that — a team saves money per video but costs hours in coordination.

### When an agency makes sense

An agency fits when your time is the bottleneck. You pay more per video for a process that already exists: a named editor, a backup editor, quality checks and specialists for graphics and sound. Ask any agency who will actually edit your videos, whether that person stays on your channel, and what happens if they leave. Vague answers are a warning sign.

### When a freelancer makes sense

A freelancer is the right first step if you're new to outsourcing, publish once a week or less, or need one specific skill. Many creators start with a freelancer, then move to a team or agency once volume grows and a single person becomes a bottleneck.

## Step 3: Where to find editors

- **Freelance marketplaces** such as Upwork and Fiverr have huge pools. Filter by portfolio and reviews, not price.
- **Creator communities** on Discord, Reddit and X often have job boards and recommendation threads.
- **Referrals** from other creators in your niche are the highest-quality source.
- **Agency websites** list case studies, process and pricing — compare a few.
- **YouTube itself.** Check the descriptions of videos you love; editors are often credited.

## Step 4: Screen portfolios properly

When reviewing portfolios, look beyond pretty visuals:

- **Niche relevance.** Has the editor worked on content like yours? Gaming, finance, podcasts and vlogs require different instincts.
- **Retention thinking.** Do the edits have strong hooks and tight pacing, or just lots of effects?
- **Audio quality.** Listen with headphones. Clean, consistent audio is a sign of professionalism.
- **Consistency.** Is quality high across the portfolio or just in one showcase video?
- **Results.** Can they share before-and-after metrics or client references?

## Step 5: Run a paid or free test

Never hire on portfolio alone. Give your top two or three candidates the same short piece of real footage and the same brief. A two- to five-minute segment is plenty.

Compare:

- How well they followed the brief.
- How strong the hook and pacing are.
- Communication: did they ask good questions?
- Turnaround: did they deliver when promised?
- Revisions: how well did they respond to feedback?

Many editing teams, including ours, offer a [free trial edit](/free-trial-edit). With freelancers, offering to pay for the test is fair and attracts better candidates.

## Step 6: Ask these questions before you commit

1. What's your standard turnaround for a first cut and for revisions?
2. How many revision rounds are included?
3. What software do you use, and can I get project files?
4. How do you handle music licensing?
5. Who owns the final video? (The answer should be: you.)
6. What happens if you're sick or on holiday?
7. How do you prefer to receive footage and feedback?
8. Do you look at analytics and suggest improvements?
9. Will you sign an NDA if needed?
10. What's your payment schedule?

## Step 7: Set up for success

The first month sets the tone for the relationship.

**Create a style guide.** Fonts, colours, caption style, music mood, zoom frequency, things you never want. Update it as you go.

**Use a simple workflow.** A shared Drive or Dropbox folder for footage, a review tool like Frame.io for time-coded feedback, and one chat channel for questions.

**Give feedback that's specific.** "At 2:14 the cut feels early — hold the reaction for another second" beats "make it more engaging."

**Share your analytics.** Retention graphs tell your editor what's working. Read our guide on [improving YouTube retention through editing](/blog/increase-youtube-audience-retention) for what to look for together.

**Batch your footage.** Sending several videos at once gives your editor flexibility and often reduces cost.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Hiring the cheapest option.** Re-editing a bad cut costs more than paying for a good one.
- **Skipping the brief.** Editors can't read your mind.
- **Micromanaging every frame.** Give direction on outcomes; let professionals handle execution.
- **No backup plan.** If your channel depends on one freelancer, know who you'd call if they disappeared.
- **Ignoring communication red flags.** Slow, vague replies during the trial rarely improve after hiring.

## The bottom line

The right editor is a creative partner who makes your videos better and your life easier. Freelancers offer flexibility and value; agencies offer reliability and breadth. Whichever you choose, define what you need, test on real footage and invest in a clear workflow.
