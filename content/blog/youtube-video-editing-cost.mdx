If you've ever asked three editors for a quote, you've probably received three wildly different numbers. One freelancer offers to edit your ten-minute video for $30. An agency quotes $400. A friend of a friend says they'll do it "for exposure." So what does YouTube video editing actually cost in 2026, and how do you know whether you're overpaying or about to get burned?

This guide breaks down typical price ranges, explains what you're paying for at each level, and gives you a simple way to budget. The figures below are broad market ranges based on public freelancer marketplaces, agency price pages and our own experience quoting hundreds of projects. Your exact price will depend on your footage, style and volume.

## The short answer

For a standard 8–15 minute YouTube video, most creators in 2026 pay somewhere in these bands:

| Editor type | Typical price per video | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entry-level freelancer | $25–$80 | Simple cuts, tight budgets, testing |
| Experienced freelancer | $80–$250 | Most growing channels |
| Specialist agency / studio | $150–$600+ | Consistency, volume, strategy |
| In-house editor (full-time) | $3,000–$7,000+ per month | Channels publishing daily |

Short-form clips (Shorts, Reels, TikToks) are usually priced lower, from roughly $15 to $80 per clip depending on complexity. Podcasts are often priced per episode, commonly $75–$300 for a multi-camera edit.

Those ranges are wide for a reason. Let's look at what moves your price up or down.

## What actually drives the cost of editing

### 1. Raw footage length versus final length

Editors spend most of their time watching and selecting. Turning 20 minutes of tidy, scripted footage into a 10-minute video is quick. Turning two hours of unscripted rambling into the same 10 minutes is a completely different job. Many editors quote on final runtime but adjust for the raw-to-final ratio, so it pays to send organised footage.

### 2. Editing style and density

A calm talking-head video with occasional B-roll and simple captions is at the lower end of any price band. A fast, high-retention edit with zooms every few seconds, sound effects, custom motion graphics, memes, animated maps and stock sourcing can take five to ten times as long. If you love a particular creator's style, ask the editor how long that style takes — it tells you a lot about the realistic price.

### 3. Motion graphics and custom animation

Templates are cheap; custom After Effects work is not. An animated chart, a branded intro or a 3D product reveal adds hours. Some editors include basic text animation in their price and charge separately for anything bespoke.

### 4. Audio and colour work

Basic noise reduction and volume levelling should be included in any professional edit. Full sound design — layered ambience, music editing to the beat, SFX timed to every cut — and true colour grading in DaVinci Resolve are skills that cost more. If your audio was recorded poorly, repair work can also add time.

### 5. Turnaround time

Need it in 24 hours? Expect to pay a rush fee, commonly 25–50% on top. Standard turnaround for most professional editors and agencies is two to four days for a first cut.

### 6. Revisions

Most quotes include one or two rounds of revisions. Unlimited revisions sound attractive but usually mean the price is padded to cover them. A clear brief upfront is the cheapest way to reduce revision rounds.

### 7. Volume and consistency

Monthly retainers almost always cost less per video than one-off projects. An editor who knows your style, fonts, music taste and audience works faster on video ten than on video one, and they pass some of that efficiency back to you.

## Freelancer, agency or in-house: what are you really paying for?

### Freelancers

Freelancers are the most flexible option. A great freelancer can be the best-value investment your channel ever makes. The trade-offs are capacity and reliability: one person gets sick, goes on holiday or takes on a bigger client, and your upload schedule slips. Quality also varies enormously between freelancers at the same price point, so trial edits and references matter.

### Agencies and studios

An agency costs more per video than a solo freelancer at the same skill level, but you're buying a system: a project manager, backup editors, quality control and usually extra services like thumbnails, Shorts cut-downs and channel strategy. For creators publishing weekly or more, the value is mostly in never missing an upload. We wrote a full comparison in [how to hire a YouTube video editor](/blog/hire-youtube-video-editor).

### In-house editors

Hiring a full-time editor makes sense once you're publishing so much content that you'd keep someone busy every day. Remember to include salary, taxes, benefits, software licences, hardware and management time. For many channels under a few hundred thousand subscribers, an outsourced team is cheaper and more flexible.

## Example budgets for common channel types

Here are a few realistic scenarios to help you budget.

**The weekly educational channel.** One 12-minute video a week with B-roll, captions and simple graphics, plus a thumbnail. With an experienced freelancer or agency monthly plan, expect roughly $500–$1,200 per month.

**The podcast with clips.** One 60-minute multi-cam episode a week, plus eight vertical clips per episode. Expect around $800–$2,000 per month, depending on clip complexity.

**The Shorts-first creator.** Twenty Shorts a month with animated captions and sound design. Often $400–$1,000 per month.

**The faceless documentary channel.** Two 15-minute videos a month with heavy stock sourcing, maps and motion graphics. Expect $400–$1,000+ per month because of research and animation time.

## How to get more value from every dollar

You can lower your editing cost without lowering quality:

- **Send organised footage.** Name files clearly, remove obvious false starts and mark the best takes. Editors charge for time.
- **Write a short brief.** Two minutes of notes on the hook, key points and call to action saves hours of guesswork.
- **Build a style guide once.** Fonts, colours, caption style, music mood and reference videos. Every future edit gets faster.
- **Batch your content.** Sending four videos at once is usually cheaper than four separate rush jobs.
- **Repurpose aggressively.** Ask for Shorts cut-downs from every long video. The marginal cost is low and the reach is high.

## Red flags when comparing quotes

Cheap isn't always a bargain. Watch out for:

- **No portfolio in your niche.** Gaming editing and finance explainer editing are different skills.
- **No mention of music licensing.** Copyright claims can demonetise a video or worse.
- **Vague turnaround.** "When it's ready" is not a schedule.
- **No trial or sample.** Any confident editor should be willing to show you a short sample on your own footage.
- **Ownership confusion.** Make sure you own the final video and can request project files.

## How pricing differs by format

YouTube is only one format. If your content plan also includes Shorts, podcasts or ads, it helps to know how those are usually priced, because the units differ.

| Format | Usual pricing unit | What moves the price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shorts, Reels, TikToks | Per clip | Caption style, motion graphics, whether clips are cut from a longer video |
| Long-form YouTube | Per video or per finished minute | Raw-to-final ratio, B-roll density, graphics |
| Video podcasts | Per episode | Number of cameras, episode length, number of clips cut from it |
| Ads (UGC, VSL) | Per ad or per campaign | Number of hooks and variations, aspect ratios, script support |
| Animation (logo, 3D) | Per project | Length, complexity, number of style rounds |

For reference, our own published starting prices are $25 per short-form clip, $79 per long-form video up to 15 minutes and $99 per podcast episode up to 60 minutes, with lower per-video rates on monthly plans. Ads and animation are quoted per project because the scope varies too much for a flat rate. These are our numbers, not a market average — use them as one data point when comparing quotes.

## Per-minute or per-video: which pricing model is fairer?

Per-video pricing is simple and predictable: you know the cost before you shoot. It works well when your videos are a consistent length and style. The risk is on the editor's side, so per-video quotes often include a length cap (for example, "up to 15 minutes").

Per-finished-minute pricing scales with the work. A four-minute explainer costs less than a twenty-minute deep dive, which is fair when your lengths vary a lot. The catch is that finished length isn't the only driver — two hours of rambling raw footage cut down to ten minutes can take longer than a tightly scripted twenty-minute video. Ask how raw footage length affects the quote either way.

Hourly pricing is common with freelancers and in-house contractors. It is the most transparent about effort but the least predictable for budgeting, so agree a cap per video before work starts.

## Is professional editing worth it?

The honest answer: it depends on what your time is worth and what the edit does for your channel. Many creators spend eight to fifteen hours editing a single video. If outsourcing frees that time for better ideas, more filming and more consistent uploads, the maths usually works out — especially when the edit itself improves retention, which compounds into more impressions over time.

The best way to find out is to test. Give a professional editor one video, compare its retention graph and click-through rate with your recent uploads, and decide based on data.
