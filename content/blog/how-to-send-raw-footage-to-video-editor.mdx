You've filmed your video. Now it needs to get to your editor — often gigabytes of files, sometimes from several cameras and a separate microphone. It sounds like the easy part, but a messy handover is one of the most common reasons edits take longer than expected: missing clips, compressed files, unclear notes and back-and-forth messages before work can even start.

This guide covers how to send raw footage to a video editor properly: which transfer method to use, how to organise and name files, what to include with the footage, and the mistakes that cost time.

## Why the handover matters

Editors can only work with what they receive. If the footage arrives compressed, the final video will look worse no matter how good the edit is. If files are unlabelled, the editor spends paid time sorting them. If a key clip is missing, the project stalls until you find it.

A good handover means the editor can open the folder and start cutting immediately.

## Step 1: Never send footage through messaging apps

WhatsApp, Telegram, Messenger and most email services compress video to save bandwidth. A sharp 4K clip can arrive as a blurry, low-bitrate file. Social apps also strip metadata and can change frame rates.

Always send the **original files straight from the camera or phone**, not exported, screen-recorded or re-saved copies.

On iPhone, AirDrop to a computer and cloud uploads from the Files app keep the original quality. Avoid sharing from the Photos app with "Most Compatible" settings if your project needs maximum quality.

## Step 2: Choose a transfer method

| Method | Good for | Things to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Google Drive | Most projects; easy sharing | Upload large batches from a computer, not the mobile app |
| Dropbox | Teams that already use it; reliable syncing | Free storage is small for video |
| WeTransfer | One-off transfers | Links expire; free tier has size limits |
| Frame.io | Review and feedback in one place | Best when your editor also uses it |
| Physical drive | Very large shoots, slow internet | Shipping time, risk of loss — keep a backup |

For ongoing work with the same editor, a shared cloud folder is the simplest option: you upload to it each week and the editor always knows where to look.

Whatever you choose, **check the upload finished**. Large uploads often fail silently when a laptop sleeps. Compare the file count and sizes in the cloud with the originals on your drive.

## Step 3: Organise the folder

A simple, consistent structure saves time on every project:

```
2026-10-22_Episode-14/
  01_Camera-A/
  02_Camera-B/
  03_Audio/
  04_Screen-Recordings/
  05_B-roll/
  06_Graphics-Logos/
  07_Music/
  README.txt
```

Not every project needs every folder. A single-camera talking head video might only have the camera files and a README. The point is that each type of file has an obvious home.

## Step 4: Name files clearly

Camera file names like `C0043.MP4` mean nothing to anyone. You don't have to rename every clip, but rename the ones that matter:

- `01_Intro_Take3_BEST.mp4`
- `02_Main-Section.mp4`
- `03_Product-Demo_Closeup.mp4`

If you film multiple takes, mark your preferred take. Your editor will thank you — and you'll spend less time on revisions.

## Step 5: Send separate audio and sync notes

If you recorded audio on a separate microphone or recorder, send those files too, along with the camera audio. Editors use the camera's scratch audio to sync the good microphone track.

A clap or slate at the start of each take makes syncing faster and more reliable. If you forgot, mention it — the editor can still sync by waveform, it just takes longer.

## Step 6: Include a short brief

Footage alone doesn't tell the editor what you want. A brief can be a few lines in a text file or message. Include:

- **The goal and audience** — who it's for and what they should do after watching.
- **Platform and format** — YouTube 16:9, Reels 9:16, or both.
- **Target length.**
- **Style references** — one or two videos you'd like it to feel like.
- **Must-keep and must-cut moments,** with timestamps if possible.
- **Brand assets** — logo, fonts, colours, intro or outro.
- **Captions** — needed or not, and in what style.
- **Deadline.**

For recurring work, write this once as a style guide and only send episode-specific notes after that. Our [talking head editing guide](/blog/talking-head-video-editing) includes a checklist you can adapt.

## Step 7: Add the extras up front

Send everything the edit needs in one go, rather than mid-project:

- Screen recordings you mention in the video.
- Product photos, logos and graphics.
- Music you've licensed, or a note to let the editor choose.
- Names and titles for lower thirds, spelled correctly.
- Links you mention, for on-screen text or end screens.

## How much footage is too much?

There's no hard limit, but more raw footage takes longer to review, and that time is part of what you pay for. If you recorded two hours to make a ten-minute video, it helps to mark the sections you know you want. Some editors price by raw footage length for this reason; our [cost guide](/blog/youtube-video-editing-cost) explains how raw-to-final ratio affects price.

## Camera settings that make editing easier

The handover starts when you press record. A few settings choices make the editor's job faster and the result better:

- **Pick one frame rate and keep it.** Mixing 24, 30 and 60 fps clips in one project causes stutter when they're combined. If you want slow motion, shoot those clips at a higher rate on purpose and tell the editor.
- **Record at the highest quality you can store.** 4K gives room to crop for punch-ins and vertical versions. If storage is tight, 1080p at a high bitrate is better than 4K at a low one.
- **Lock exposure and focus** for talking head shots, so brightness doesn't pulse when you move.
- **Leave a little headroom and side room.** Framing slightly wider than you think lets the editor reframe for 9:16 later.
- **Record room tone.** Ten seconds of silence in the room helps the editor patch gaps and clean noise.
- **Check the audio before the full take.** Listen back to a short test with headphones. Fixing echo or a loose microphone at the shoot takes seconds; fixing it in the edit is sometimes impossible.

## Phone footage: specific tips

Many creators film entirely on phones, which is fine — modern phones produce excellent footage. A few phone-specific issues come up often:

- **HDR and Dolby Vision.** iPhones record HDR by default. It can look washed out or overly bright on some editing systems and platforms. Either turn it off in camera settings for consistency, or tell your editor so they can handle the conversion.
- **Variable frame rate.** Phones sometimes vary frame rate to save power, which can cause audio drift over long recordings. Using a dedicated camera app with a fixed frame rate avoids it.
- **Storage settings.** On iPhone, "High Efficiency" saves HEVC files that are smaller but harder for some systems to edit. They're still original quality, so send them as they are — just don't convert them yourself.
- **Lens choice.** The ultra-wide lens distorts faces. Use the main lens for talking head shots.

## How long should an editor have?

Give your editor enough time to do the job well. Turnaround depends on length and complexity, but rushing almost always costs quality. If you publish on a schedule, agree a regular delivery day — footage uploaded by Monday, first cut by Wednesday, final by Friday, for example — so neither side is working against a surprise deadline.

## Privacy and ownership

Raw footage can include private information, unreleased products or client material. Share folders only with the people working on the project, use links that require sign-in where possible, and remove access when the project ends. Agree in writing who owns the final video and project files. A professional editor or agency should be clear that you own your footage and the finished edit.

## Common mistakes

- **Sending compressed files** from messaging apps or social downloads.
- **Partial uploads** that stopped when the computer went to sleep.
- **No audio files** when a separate microphone was used.
- **Mixed projects in one folder** with no way to tell which clips belong where.
- **Feedback spread across channels** — some by email, some by text, some by voice note. Keep notes in one place.
- **Deleting originals** before the final video is approved. Keep a backup until delivery.

## A handover checklist

Before you send the link, check:

1. Original, uncompressed files from the camera and audio recorder.
2. A clear folder structure and named key clips.
3. Preferred takes marked.
4. A brief with goal, platform, length, references and deadline.
5. Brand assets, music and screen recordings included.
6. Upload complete and link permissions set.
7. A local backup kept.
